Why More Content Isn't the Answer: Better Coverage Is

AI made publishing trivial. It also made prioritization the only part that still matters.

By Jim Umlauf, Founder, VEWO  ·  September 17, 2026  ·  11 min read

TL;DR

AI dramatically reduced the cost of producing content. That's useful — and it also makes it easier than ever to publish enormous amounts of content that accomplishes very little.

A better strategy starts with coverage, not volume. Which questions matter to the buying journey? Which do you already answer well? Which answers are weak, buried, or outdated? Then decide the right way to close each gap: an article, a product page, an FAQ, a comparison, a buying guide, or a refresh of something you published two years ago.

Content is the tool. Coverage is the objective.

Nobody Needs 50 More Mediocre Blog Posts

AI has made content creation trivial. Give a tool a topic, ask for an article, publish, repeat. A company that struggled to publish two articles a month can theoretically publish 50.

Here's the problem. More content doesn't necessarily mean more visibility, more authority, or more help for customers. And it certainly doesn't mean you're answering the questions that matter to your business.

The internet already has plenty of content. What most brands need isn't more of it — they need better coverage. They need to know what customers are trying to learn, where their website answers those questions well, where it doesn't, and which gaps are worth closing.

Sometimes the answer is a new article. Sometimes it's a product page, a comparison, an FAQ, a buying guide, or a collection page. And sometimes it's improving something you published two years ago.

Stop measuring content by how much you publish. Start measuring how many important questions you answer well.

1 The Wrong Question

How Much Content Should We Publish?

How many blogs a month? Five? Ten? Twenty? What's the magic number? There isn't one.

It's like asking how many products you should launch this year. First you'd want to know what the customer needs, where the gaps are, what's already working, and what you can execute well. Content deserves the same treatment.

AI made creation easier. It didn't make strategy easier.

Creating the words got easier, so deciding which words need to exist became more important.

Anyone can ask AI for 100 blog ideas about dog travel, then have it write all 100. But should those 100 articles exist? Do customers ask those questions? Are they relevant to your products? Does your site already answer them? Are competitors dominating those conversations? Would an article even be the right format?

The shift

Production has become cheap. Prioritization has become more valuable.

Start with questions, not content

Don't start by asking what content you should create. Start with: what questions need better answers?

Say you're an outdoor apparel company. Customers are asking whether merino wool works in hot weather, whether it shrinks, how it compares with polyester, whether it's itchy, how to wash it, whether it's worth the price, and what to pack for a three-day trip.

Now you have an information landscape. The next step isn't "write 10 blogs." It's decide the best way to answer each question.

2 The Format

Not Every Question Needs a Blog Post

This is where content strategies go wrong. Everything becomes a blog. But an article isn't always the best home for an answer.

If the question is…The right format is usually…Why
"Will this fit a 2025 Ford F-150?"Product page improvementDon't make someone read 1,500 words to find a fitment answer
"Can this go in the washing machine?"FAQQuestion, answer, done
"What's the difference between standard and premium?"ComparisonMake the differences obvious and help them choose
"Which of your products fits my situation?"Collection or category pageExplain the category and help them navigate options
"What should I look for when buying one?"Buying guideThe subject genuinely requires depth
"Why does this happen, and what are my options?"New articleNeeds explanation, nuance, examples, and evidence

That last row gets overlooked constantly. Maybe you published a great article three years ago. It ranks. It has backlinks. It has traffic. But it's outdated, incomplete, or poorly structured. Why start over?

Content strategy should include maintenance, not just production. Most brands have no line item for improving what already exists.

3 The Diagnosis

Think in Terms of Information Gaps

Imagine all the important questions surrounding your brand. Then imagine the answers currently available on your website. The space between the two is your information gap, and your job is to systematically close it.

But gaps aren't all the same kind of problem:

Size gapCustomers ask, and your site says almost nothing
Findability gapYou have the answer, but it's buried
Quality gapYou answer it; a competitor answers it better
Freshness gapIt was correct two years ago
Authority gapYou make claims without evidence
Context gapYou explain what it is, not who it's for

Those are six different problems. Publishing another random blog doesn't reliably fix any of them.

4 The Map

Coverage Follows the Buying Journey

Not every question has equal value. Someone asking "what is a dog seat cover?" is at a very different point than someone asking "what's the best seat cover for a 90-pound dog if I still need half the back seat for a child?"

Both matter. The second contains far more context and far more purchase intent. That's why coverage should map to the journey — and why it helps to see your category as a map rather than a list.

StageWhat they're asking
Problem awarenessWhy does this happen? Do I need a solution at all?
Category educationWhat types of solutions exist? What should I look for?
Product evaluationWhich features matter? What size? Will it work for me?
ComparisonWhat's the difference? Why does one cost more?
TrustCan I believe these claims? Has it been tested? What do customers say?
PurchaseCost, delivery, warranty, returns
OwnershipHow do I install it, clean it, get the best results, or get help?

Better coverage helps humans and machines

This matters more as AI plays a larger role in discovery. If someone asks for the best seat protector for a large muddy dog when one rear seat must stay free for a passenger, the system needs to reason about product type, dog size, mud protection, passenger requirements, seat configuration, capabilities, limitations, and alternatives.

An article titled "10 Reasons Dogs Are Great Travel Companions" doesn't help. Neither do 50 more like it. What helps is strong information around the specific questions that matter.

5 The Source

Authority Comes From Depth, Not Volume

There's another reason we don't believe in flooding a website with AI-generated content: volume creates the appearance of activity without demonstrating expertise.

Authority comes from what you know. What have you learned operating in the category? What have you tested? What do customers consistently tell you? What claims can you prove? What nuances do outsiders miss? What did you learn after selling hundreds of thousands of products?

That's where the good content lives. AI can help you research, organize, draft, and repurpose it — but the underlying value has to come from real knowledge. The objective isn't to make the website look busy. It's to make it useful.

More content can create new problems

Publishing indiscriminately creates clutter:

  • Duplicate intent. Multiple articles answering nearly the same question.
  • Contradictions. Old content disagreeing with newer information.
  • Internal competition. Thin pages competing against one another.
  • Decay. Outdated product information and broken internal links.
  • Abandonment. Hundreds of pages nobody maintains.

Eventually the content library becomes another thing nobody wants to manage. A good content system should become more useful over time, not simply larger.

6 The Scorecard

Measure Coverage, Not Publishing Activity

This requires changing the scorecard. Instead of celebrating "we published 20 articles this month," ask:

MeasureThe question it answers
Questions coveredHow many important customer questions do we now answer well?
Remaining gapsWhich commercially important questions still lack strong answers?
Existing contentWhich pages improved, which declined, which need updating?
Search visibilityWhere are we gaining impressions and rankings, and where do competitors still win?
AI visibilityFor important questions, is our brand mentioned, referenced, or absent?
Journey coverageAre we improving across awareness, evaluation, comparison, trust, purchase, and ownership?
Business impactAre customers engaging with it? Is it supporting conversions? Do sales and support use it?

Now you're measuring something much closer to the actual objective.

7 The Cadence

Why We Think in 90-Day Sprints

This is exactly why our approach runs on 90-day cycles. Don't build a giant 12-month calendar in January and execute it blindly until December — too much changes. Customer behavior, search results, competitors, AI systems, product launches, and the performance of your own pages.

Identify. Understand the current landscape. What questions matter? Where are the gaps? What already performs? Where are competitors stronger? Where is your brand absent? Then prioritize.

Execute. Close the highest-value gaps — a new article, a product page improvement, a comparison, expanded FAQs, a buying guide, a better collection page, a refresh, stronger internal links, or adding evidence and original expertise.

Measure. What gained visibility and what didn't? What started ranking? Which questions produce impressions? Where do AI systems mention the brand? What are customers actually using?

Reprioritize. Something worked faster than expected — build around it. An article isn't moving — improve it. A new competitor appeared — investigate. Customer questions shifted — adjust.

Content should earn its place

Before creating something new, ask: What question does this answer? Why does that question matter? Who's asking it? Where does it sit in the buying journey? Do we already answer it? Could an existing page answer it better? What can we add that isn't already everywhere else? How will we know whether this improved our coverage?

Permission

If you can't answer those, maybe you don't need another piece of content. Sometimes publishing less and improving what you have is the better strategy.

8 Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

So how many articles should we publish per month?

Whatever number of real gaps you can close well and sustain for a year. For most brands that lands between four and ten pieces of work per month — but some of that work should be improving existing pages, not publishing new ones.

How do we find our information gaps?

Start with the questions customers actually ask in search, support tickets, sales calls, reviews, and community discussions. Then check what your site says about each one, what competitors say, and whether AI systems mention you when asked.

Is it better to refresh old content or publish new content?

Refresh first when the page already has rankings, links, or traffic and the gap is quality, freshness, or structure. Publish new when the question genuinely has no home on your site.

Won't publishing less hurt our SEO?

Not if what you publish is better and your existing pages keep improving. Thin, duplicative pages that compete with each other and go unmaintained tend to create more problems than they solve.

9 Conclusion

Stop Counting Articles

AI has made content production easier than at any point in history. That doesn't mean brands should publish more simply because they can.

The companies that benefit most won't be the ones generating the most pages. They'll be the ones systematically building the most useful information foundation around the questions that matter to their customers.

That means creating when creation is necessary. Updating when updating is smarter. Adding an FAQ when an FAQ solves the problem. Improving a product page when the answer belongs there. Building a comparison when customers need help choosing. And sometimes doing nothing, because the question is already answered exceptionally well.

Content is the tool. Coverage is the objective.

Stop asking how much content you published. Start asking how many important questions you answer well.
